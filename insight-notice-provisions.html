The 21-day trap: notice provisions that quietly kill your claim

A subcontractor in Pasadena lost an $8M delay claim last year. Not because the delay was not real, and not because the schedule analysis was weak — but because a project manager typed “owner caused 14 days late, awaiting mock-up” into a daily report and nobody ever sent a notice.

Why the clause bites harder than it looks

Notice conditions in construction contracts are drafted to look procedural. They read like housekeeping: submit a written claim within 21 days of the event, or the claim is waived. But their legal effect is not procedural at all. In most Texas contracts — and in the standard AIA and ConsensusDocs forms as usually modified — a properly drafted notice provision is a condition precedent. Miss it and the merits never get read.

Three features make these clauses unusually dangerous in practice:

  • They start running from knowledge, not from impact. The clock typically begins when the contractor knew or should have known of the condition, not when the cost is understood. On a job with 300 active RFIs, that is a moving target that favours the party holding the document control log.
  • They demand content, not just a warning. Many require a description of the event, the reason it is compensable or time-extending, and a preliminary estimate. “Heads up, this is going to cost us” is not notice; it is a text message.
  • They are rarely negotiated after a claim. Owners seldom waive them retroactively, and their insurers certainly will not. A $200M project can end up argued about a four-line letter.
Practical point. If you read one clause on every subcontract before mobilisation, read the claims clause. Everything else in the document can usually be fixed later; this one has a deadline attached to it.

The three questions we ask first

When a client brings a delay or changed-conditions problem, the file review starts with the same three questions, before anybody talks about damages.

  1. What exactly does the contract require, and from whom to whom? Some forms require notice to the owner through the architect; others require it to the construction manager within 48 hours, with a copy to the owner, or it is void. Compliance with the addressee is as important as compliance with the date.
  2. When did knowledge occur, and what proves it? Daily reports, meeting minutes, email traffic, weather logs, and the transmittal system all create a record that cuts both ways. We build a chronology of the client’s knowledge on day one because the other side will do the same.
  3. Is there an anti-waiver or continuing-damages provision to fight with? Many contracts contain a mutual-notice or “no waiver by conduct” clause that can be turned around, and a substantial number give separate notice periods for time and for cost.

A fifteen-minute contract audit

You do not need outside counsel to do the first pass. Give the contract to a project engineer and ask for these six answers on one page:

  • Notice window. Days from the event; days from the end of the event; days for cost, separately.
  • Required addressee and method. Certified mail, email, or Aconex/Procore transmittal only.
  • Content requirements. Description, reason, estimate, supporting documents.
  • Consequence of failure. Waiver, “condition precedent”, or a looser “shall be evidence of” formulation.
  • Concurrent-delay language. Whether the clause disclaims time or money where two causes overlap.
  • Flow-down. Whether your notice obligation to the owner is longer than your sub’s obligation to you — the gap is where money disappears.

The notice was late: four ways out

A missed window is close, not fatal. Before we advise a client to discount a claim, we look for the four escape routes that actually work at mediation.

1. Actual knowledge

If the owner’s representative wrote the delay into the superintendent meeting minutes on the same week, argue that the purpose of notice was met. This is not a slam dunk — some clauses are drafted so strictly that knowledge does not save you — but it moves value.

2. Continuing events

Where disruption is ongoing, the window may reset or run from the end of the condition. Repeated late submittal reviews are a classic example; a fresh event each week is a fresh notice each week.

3. Waiver by conduct

Where the owner has already evaluated and paid three similar notices under the same clause, it becomes harder to say the fourth was fatally defective. Send the pattern in writing.

4. Reframe the theory

Sometimes a claim pleaded as a notice-conditioned change is stronger as a breach of the duty not to hinder, or as a constructive acceleration. Different clause, different clock.

What not to do. Do not send a bare “reservation of rights” email to stop the clock and then never follow it with the estimate. Courts read those as exactly what they are, and a series of them looks worse than silence.

What we tell clients to do on Monday

  1. Make notice a job-site habit, not a legal task. Put the window on the cover sheet of the daily report template and give the superintendent a one-page form.
  2. Over-notice. A short, well-documented letter that turns out to be unnecessary costs a paragraph of time. A missing one can cost eight figures.
  3. Attach the number, even a bad one. A preliminary estimate satisfies most content requirements and starts the clock on the other side’s obligation to respond.
  4. Calendar the sub-tier window. If your subcontract with the curtain-wall installer gives you 14 days to pass through a claim and the owner’s prime contract gives you 21, you are already out of time on the day you learn about the event.
  5. Send us the clause before the problem. We will mark it up in an hour, and it is a great hour to spend.

Key takeaways

  • Notice provisions are conditions precedent, not paperwork. Treat the date as a hard deadline.
  • Content and addressee matter as much as timing.
  • Late is not lost: knowledge, continuing events, waiver and re-theorising all recover value.
  • Align the clock from your subcontract up to your prime contract, not the reverse.

This article is general information for a Texas audience and is not legal advice on any specific facts. Statutory notice periods (including Chapter 53 liens and Chapter 2251 claims) have their own deadlines that no contract can vary — check them independently.
