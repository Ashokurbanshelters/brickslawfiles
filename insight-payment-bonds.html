Payment bonds after the 2025 amendments: what changed for subcontractors

Claim-by-presentation is now the default on most public work in Texas. For a material supplier that used to survive on a notarised letter sent late, the margin has gone to zero. Here is what actually moved.

What changed, in one paragraph

The 2025 amendments to the state bonding scheme tightened three things at once: the content required in a claim, the method and timing of presentation, and the documentation a claimant must attach to be heard at all. Nothing about the underlying obligation moved — the principal still owes, the surety still answers within the penalty — but the procedural gate in front of that obligation got narrower, and gates are where these cases are decided.

If you are a second- or third-tier supplier, the practical consequence is simple: the claim is now drafted when the job starts, not when the money stops.

The five shifts that decide claims

1. Presentation must be provable, not merely sent

Certified mail with return receipt remains the safe route, and email is no longer sufficient on its own even where the contract permits it. Keep the receipt with the affidavit; an envelope you cannot produce is a claim you cannot prove.

2. The claim must attach, not reference

Invoice copies, delivery tickets and contract excerpts are now expected with the claim rather than promised in response to a request. Assemble the package on the day you decide to claim — a claim bolted together three weeks later is usually filed on the merits and lost on the paperwork.

3. Notice to the owner still matters on funded work

Even where the bond route is open, contractual notice to the general contractor and, on funded projects, to the public authority is what preserves leverage and keeps payment flowing on your other jobs. Do not treat the bond as the only letter you send.

4. Release language is being read literally

Conditional releases that quietly behaved as unconditional ones, and progress waivers signed “for value received”, are now the first exhibit a surety puts in front of the arbitrator. Sign nothing that does not say “contingent on receipt of funds”.

5. The 90-day clock is still the clock

Nothing in the amendments extended the statutory window. The day your work finished — not the day the job was “substantially complete” on the general contractor’s definition — starts it, and on multi-lot work it can run separately for each lot.

Our practice change. Every bond-eligible client now gets a two-page claims pack at mobilisation: a claim template, an exhibit checklist, and a deadline calendar counted backwards from expected completion. It costs nothing, and it has saved more claims than any argument we have made since.

Read the surety like a claim handler

Sureties are not trying to win a legal argument; they are managing a reserve. A claim that arrives complete — correct form, attached exhibits, defensible arithmetic from invoice to amount owed — gets a settlement conversation quickly, because the adjuster can price it. A claim that arrives as a demand letter with a stack of unindexed PDFs gets a denial and a request for more, and the clock keeps running while you respond.

  • State the amount and show the math on one page.
  • Separate contract balance, retainage, change work, and interest.
  • Identify the fund you are claiming against, expressly.
  • Enclose the subcontract, the invoices and the delivery evidence, in that order.

Bond-claim readiness checklist

  • Contract and bond number. Get the bond from the general contractor in writing before you ship material.
  • Completion date, per lot. The clock can run separately for each site on a multi-lot job.
  • Exhibit pack. Invoices, tickets, signed deliveries, change directives, correspondence.
  • Release review. Check every waiver signed across the whole job, not just the disputed month.
  • Collectability. If the principal is insolvent, the bond is the case. Treat it that way from day one.

Key takeaways

  • Completeness now beats speed on presentation — but never at the cost of the deadline.
  • Attach, do not reference.
  • Read your signed releases before you budget the recovery.
  • Write the claim for the adjuster, not for the arbitrator.
